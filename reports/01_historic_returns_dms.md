# Historic Investment Returns: The Dimson–Marsh–Staunton (DMS) Dataset

*This report was compiled from publicly available sources as of June 2026. The most recent edition of the underlying dataset is the UBS Global Investment Returns Yearbook 2026 (covering 1900–2025). Because the full Yearbook is a paid/subscriber publication, several figures below are drawn from the public summary edition, the authors' academic papers, and reputable secondary reporting; vintages and windows are stated throughout. All specific figures should be checked against the primary Yearbook and the underlying DMS Database before they are relied upon in a regulatory determination.*

---

## 1. What the dataset is

The Dimson–Marsh–Staunton ("DMS") dataset is the most widely used long-run record of global financial-asset returns. It was created by **Professor Elroy Dimson, Professor Paul Marsh and Dr Mike Staunton**, all associated with **London Business School (LBS)**; Marsh and Staunton are Emeritus Professors at LBS, and Dimson is Emeritus Professor at LBS who moved his primary affiliation to the **University of Cambridge** (Cambridge Judge Business School), where he is Research Director and chairs the Centre for Endowment Asset Management [1][2][3].

The dataset's public debut was the 2002 book **_Triumph of the Optimists: 101 Years of Global Investment Returns_** (Princeton University Press), which assembled consistent return series for equities, bonds, bills, currencies and inflation across 16 countries from 1900 to the start of the twenty-first century [4][5]. The project had grown out of the ABN AMRO–LBS "Millennium Book" research of 2000–2001 [6].

Since 2000 the dataset has been refreshed annually as the **Global Investment Returns Yearbook**. The publisher has changed twice. Early editions appeared under **ABN AMRO**; from the **2010** edition the **Credit Suisse Research Institute** became publisher [7]; and after **UBS acquired Credit Suisse in 2023** (announced 19 March 2023, completed 12 June 2023) [8], the **2024 edition became the first UBS-branded Yearbook**, described by UBS as "brought to you by UBS for the first time, while reflecting the continuity of the long-standing collaboration between its primary authors and Credit Suisse" [9][10]. The **latest edition is the UBS Global Investment Returns Yearbook 2026**, published in March 2026 and covering **1900–2025** [11][12].

This long, consistent, multi-country pedigree is precisely why the dataset has become the reference source for the historical Total Market Return (TMR) and Equity Risk Premium (ERP) in regulatory cost-of-capital work.

## 2. Coverage

**Markets.** The 2026 edition reports data for **35 markets** plus **five composite indices** — the World, World ex-US, Europe, Developed markets and Emerging markets [11][13]. Of the 35 markets, **23 have continuous histories back to 1900** (the legacy figure was "21", which is why earlier write-ups cite 21 markets); the remaining 12 begin in the second half of the twentieth century [13][14]. The underlying equity indices extend coverage to roughly **90 countries**, which the authors describe as effectively 100% of investable global equities, with the 35 core markets covering the great majority of world market capitalisation [15].

**Time span.** The series begins in **1900**. The 2026 edition therefore spans **126 years (1900–2025)**; the 2025 edition spanned 125 years (1900–2024) [11][16].

**Asset classes.** For each market the dataset provides annual real and nominal returns on **equities, long-term government bonds and treasury bills**, together with **inflation and currencies**; **gold** was added in recent editions, and the Yearbook regularly publishes evidence on **factor/style premia** (size, value, income/yield, momentum, low volatility) [11][17]. Returns are total returns with income reinvested [4].

## 3. Headline long-run real returns and risk premia

The headline measures are **real (inflation-adjusted) geometric (compound) means**. Geometric means answer "what did a buy-and-hold investor actually compound at?"; arithmetic means (always higher for volatile series) are discussed separately in Section 4.

A point of frequent confusion must be flagged. The 2026 launch coverage led with the **United States** figures — real equity returns of **6.6% p.a.** for 1900–2025, versus **1.6%** for US bonds, with USD 1 invested in US equities in 1900 growing to **USD 3,296 in real terms** (and USD 124,854 nominal) by end-2025 [12][18]. These are **US** numbers, not world numbers. The **World** index real return is materially lower, at roughly **5.0–5.2% p.a.** in the most recent editions [16][19].

The most recently published World figures are:

- **World real equity return:** ≈ **5.2% p.a.** (1900–2024, 2025 edition); ≈ 5.0% on the 1900–2022 window [16][19].
- **World real bond return:** ≈ **1.7% p.a.**; **World real bill return:** ≈ **0.5% p.a.** [16][19].
- **World ERP relative to bills:** ≈ **4.3–4.6% p.a.** (geometric); **relative to bonds:** ≈ **3.1–3.3% p.a.** [16][19][20].

These are consistent with the authors' peer-reviewed work: in "Equity Premia Around the World" (19-country World index, 1900–2010) the World real equity return was **5.5%**, the ERP versus bills **4.5%** and versus bonds **3.8%** [21].

**Country detail.** From the original book (1900–2000, geometric real): the **US returned 6.7%**, the **UK 5.8%**, **Japan 4.5%** and **Germany 3.6%**, with **Sweden the best at 7.6%** and **Belgium the worst at 2.5%** [23]. The latest-edition US figure (6.6%, 1900–2025) is essentially unchanged. On recent windows the best-performing equity markets since 1900 are **Australia (≈6.4%)** and the **US (≈6.4%)**, followed by Denmark, South Africa and New Zealand [24]. Critically, in the latest editions **every market with a continuous history since 1900 — Germany included — has delivered a positive real equity return** [14].

**Germany** is the canonical illustration of why a global, not US-centric, record matters. Germany endured two world wars and the **1922–23 hyperinflation**, when annual inflation reached the order of hundreds of billions of percent. Equity holders fared comparatively well in real terms over the full century (≈3.6% in the book), but **fixed-income holders were wiped out**: Germany is one of several markets (with Japan, Italy, France and Belgium) showing **negative real returns on both bonds and bills** over the century [25]. German bill and bond investors lost effectively everything in 1923, and German bondholders lost a further ~92% in real terms after the Second World War [25]. Because the hyperinflation distorts the statistics so severely, DMS explicitly **exclude the years 1922–23 for Germany** when computing bond and bill returns — "Bond and bill statistics for Germany exclude the years 1922–23," and charts "omit 1922–23 for Germany" [26]. Their German equity-premium series is likewise computed over 109 years excluding 1922–23 [21].

## 4. Methodology

**Real versus nominal.** DMS emphasise **real** returns for long-run, cross-country comparison, because inflation experiences differ enormously across markets and eras (Germany's hyperinflation being the extreme case) [4][25]. Headline figures are therefore quoted in real terms throughout.

**Geometric versus arithmetic.** The geometric mean is the realised compound rate — the correct measure for **describing past multi-period performance**. The arithmetic mean is higher for any volatile series; DMS illustrate the gap with a simple example: two equally likely returns of +25% and −20% have an **arithmetic mean of 2.5% but a geometric mean of 0%** [27]. For equities with annual standard deviations around 17–20%, the **arithmetic-minus-geometric gap is of the order of 1.5–2 percentage points** (approximately one-half the variance). The choice matters for cost of capital: in principle the **arithmetic mean is the unbiased estimator of next period's expected return** and so is the textbook input for single-period discounting, but for multi-year horizons — and because the arithmetic mean of a finite sample is itself an upward-biased estimator of long-horizon compound wealth — practitioners and regulators typically use a blend or lean toward the geometric figure [27][28]. UK regulators address this by estimating a TMR range that spans both bases (see Section 5).

**Survivorship and success bias — the "Triumph of the Optimists" thesis.** The dataset's defining contribution is the correction of two biases. First, **survivorship bias**: indices that quietly drop failed companies or markets overstate returns. Second, **success bias**: extrapolating from the United States — an unusually successful market chosen with hindsight — overstates what a global investor could have expected. Princeton's own description states that "long-term stock returns are in most countries seriously overestimated, due to a focus on periods that with hindsight are known to have been successful" [29]. DMS deliberately built **survivor-bias-free indices** spanning markets that did badly — including those that suffered war, confiscation and hyperinflation (Germany, Japan, Italy, Austria) — while acknowledging that even their record omits the handful of markets (e.g. Russia 1917, China 1949) where investors lost everything, an omission that, if anything, still flatters the global average [30][31]. The book's title captures the irony: equity optimists who stayed invested were ultimately rewarded — "the optimists triumphed" — but the very record that vindicates them is selected from surviving, successful markets, so optimism looks wiser in hindsight than it was ex ante [32].

**Historical (ex-post) versus forward-looking (ex-ante) premia.** DMS argue that the **realised historical premium overstates the prospective premium**, because part of historical equity returns came from a non-repeatable **re-rating** — falling dividend yields and rising valuations as the required premium itself declined. As they put it, "the mean equity premium will overstate the prospective risk premium … because historical returns are inflated by past repricings that were triggered by a reduction in the risk premium" [33]. Decomposing realised returns into dividend growth, the dividend yield and multiple expansion, they estimate a **prospective World equity premium of around 3–3.5% on a geometric basis (≈4.5–5% arithmetic)** — roughly a percentage point below the historical figure [27][34]. Their 2003 country estimates put the forward-looking premium (vs bills) at about **4.3% for the US, 3.9% for the UK and 3.5% for the World**, against historical comparators of 5.3%, 4.2% and 4.5% respectively [22].

## 5. Regulatory relevance

DMS data are the empirical backbone of network cost-of-capital decisions in the UK and, increasingly, continental Europe. Regulators implement a CAPM in which the **TMR** (and hence the **ERP = TMR − risk-free rate**) is anchored on long-run history.

**TMR-based versus ERP-based framing.** UK regulators favour a **"TMR-based"** approach: estimate the **total** market return directly from long-run history (which appears comparatively stable across mature markets), then derive the ERP as a **balancing figure** against the prevailing risk-free rate. This is preferred to an **"ERP-based"** approach (estimate a stable ERP directly and add it to today's risk-free rate), because the empirical evidence for a stable ERP is weaker than for a stable TMR. The approach was codified by the UK Regulators Network (UKRN) study led by Wright, Burns, Mason and Pickford and the subsequent UKRN cost-of-capital guidance [35][36].

**Ofgem (RIIO).** Ofgem uses DMS as its **primary source** for the TMR, cross-checked with the Bank of England dividend-discount model. In **RIIO-2 and RIIO-ED2** the TMR was set at **6.25–6.75% CPIH-real (midpoint 6.5%)** [37][38]. For **RIIO-3** Ofgem raised the range to **6.5–7%**, assigning **equal weight to ex-ante and ex-post estimates**; in the Final Determinations (December 2025) the ex-post TMR was assessed at **about 7%**, with the allowed CPIH-real cost of equity around **5.7% for electricity transmission and 6.12% for gas distribution/transmission** — with companies and Ofgem both citing the latest DMS Yearbook [39][40].

**Ofwat (PR24).** For the 2024 water price review, Ofwat retained a **fixed, DMS-anchored TMR**, deriving an ex-post range with a midpoint near **6.9% CPIH-real** and inferring the ERP as a balancing item; the CEPA advice references the DMS "Global Investment Returns Yearbook" directly, and the Final Determination set a CPIH-real cost of equity of about **5.1%** [41][42].

**CMA.** In the 2021 **PR19 water redeterminations** the Competition and Markets Authority worked from the same DMS dataset (Ofwat had used the 2019 Credit Suisse Yearbook), estimating the TMR from long-run historical returns using arithmetic, Blume and JKM estimators and arriving at a TMR around **5.85–6.5% RPI-real** [43][44]. In the parallel **RIIO-2 energy appeals** the CMA **upheld Ofgem's cost of equity**, leaving the DMS-anchored TMR in place [45].

**Germany and continental Europe.** The **Bundesnetzagentur (BNetzA)** sets the regulated equity return (Eigenkapitalzinssatz) via a CAPM in which the market risk premium (Marktrisikoprämie) is derived from **long-run historical data using the worldwide DMS series** — explicitly the "Credit Suisse Global Investment Returns Yearbook 2021," covering up to 90 countries over 1900–2020 — taking the **mean of the geometric and arithmetic** historical MRP [46][47]. For the fourth regulatory period (decided October 2021) BNetzA set a pre-tax equity return of **5.07% for new assets and 3.51% for existing assets** [48]. The technical basis was provided by **expert reports (Gutachten) authored by Professors Otto Randl and Josef Zechner (WU Vienna) together with Frontier Economics**, which use **DMS as their primary source** for the historical MRP and derive a DMS-based MRP range of **6.1–7.3%** before adjustments for real-exchange-rate changes, valuation changes and dividend yields [49][50]. The reliance on DMS history was contested — DMS themselves were commissioned to prepare a rebuttal for one network operator, and the Düsseldorf Higher Regional Court initially criticised BNetzA for relying on historical data "without supplementary plausibility checks," though the determination was subsequently upheld at the Federal Court of Justice [50][51]. Randl and Zechner applied the same DMS-based methodology in the parallel Austrian (E-Control) determination [52].

## 6. Caveats and criticisms for regulatory use

The dataset's authority does not make its application mechanical; several judgement points recur in determinations and appeals.

- **Long-run versus recent windows.** Twenty-first-century returns have been lower for equities and higher for bonds than the twentieth-century average — the 2026 edition reports a World real equity return of only **3.5%** since 2000, against the ~5% full-history figure [53]. The choice of estimation window can therefore move the TMR by more than a percentage point.
- **Averaging convention.** Whether to anchor on the geometric mean, the arithmetic mean, or a Blume/JKM blend is genuinely consequential given the ~1.5–2 point gap; UK regulators span both, the CMA leant arithmetic, and BNetzA takes a simple geometric–arithmetic average [27][44][46].
- **Real versus nominal anchoring.** Results depend on the inflation series and the deflator (RPI vs CPIH), and on the RPI–CPIH "wedge" used to convert index-linked gilt yields. The CMA used a 0.9% wedge; DMS have progressively re-based their own back-cast inflation series toward CPI [43][37].
- **Ex-post overstates ex-ante.** As DMS stress, the realised premium embeds a non-repeatable re-rating, so a purely historical anchor risks overstating forward-looking returns — the core argument for forward-looking cross-checks [33].
- **Global average versus a single country and period.** A World historical average may not be the right prior for one country (e.g. Germany's idiosyncratic war-and-hyperinflation history) or one five-year control period; this "applicability" point underlay the German litigation and remains the central methodological debate in regulatory cost-of-capital practice [50][51].

In short, the DMS dataset is the indispensable empirical starting point for TMR and ERP — uniquely long, broad and bias-corrected — but it sets a range, not a number. The determination still turns on window, averaging convention, deflator and the weight given to forward-looking evidence.
